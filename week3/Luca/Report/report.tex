\section{Discussion of model}
**Brief description of the model

A stagnation point in the trailing edge inviscid flow causes a strong adverse pressure gradient not found empirically. Therefore, it is not enforced in the model. Instead, the extrapolated velocities above and below the leading edge are averaged, to give a more realistic result.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limit}

The pressure distribution is calculated entirely from inviscid potential, through a Bernouilli relation with the circulation-derived surface velocities. In reality, the boundary layer displacement thickness affects the outer flow curvature, however no feedback from the boundary layer calculations is implemented. This means that the variation of lift with boundary layer progression (and subsequent stall) is not captured. The effect of laminar separation bubbles on the pressure distribution is also not included.

The modelling of transition is also unsophisticated, relying on a simple empirical criterion (Eppler and Somers) that uses only local flow properties. A more physically robust approach is the $e^{n}$ method, which is based on linear stability theory: it tracks the growth of small disturbances (Tollmien–Schlichting waves) as they travel downstream and assumes that transition occurs once these disturbances have amplified by a factor of $e^{n}$. The critical $n$ value depends on the initial turbulence of the flow, typically $n=9$ for wind-tunnel conditions. 

The Thwaites' method is used for laminar flow, which describes the boundary layer shape using a single parameter `m'. However, a two parameter method, such as Head's method \cite{boundarylayersyoung} would be more appropriate. The *current model* is sensitive to curvature changes and behaves poorly in adverse pressure gradients \cite{oneparameterbad}. It also requires that flow in a laminar separation bubble transitions immediately, as it is not possible to model a separated region using the single-parameter laminar model. However, the position of transition within the bubble has a significant effect on the reattachment position, especially in low Reynolds flow \cite{lowspeeddesign}. Therefore, information is lost by assuming that it occurs at laminar separation.

Compressibility effects are not included, therefore the model is only applicable below Mach 0.3. For the high Reynolds number design case ($Re =2 \cdot 10^7$) this requires a very large aircraft.

\subsection{Comparison with XFoil}

Xfoil is an example of an industry-standard panel method solver. Due to its coupled viscous-inviscid calculations, use of the $e^n$ method for transition tests, two-parameter laminar model and improved treatment of laminar separation bubbles \cite{xfoil}, Xfoil overcomes many of the limitations of our model. Boundary layer growth is accounted for using sources, stall is modelled, and compressibility corrections improve accuracy at higher Mach numbers. It can therefore provide a comparison for model results, although its most useful feature is inverse design: the determination of the airfoil shape that produces a given pressure or velocity distribution.

\subsection{Improvements to \texttt{wasg}}\label{sec:wasg}

Initial attempts to use \texttt{wasg} were challenging, as any `bumpiness' introduced by spline manipulation triggered laminar separation. 

A least-squares spline tool was implemented to resolve this, however the non-monotonic y-x variation was problematic. Splitting the upper and lower surfaces into different splines prevented leading edge smoothness, and fixing this removed the leading and trailing edge position constraints. As an alternative, we reduced the number of spline `knots' to increase smoothness; this also helped to smooth the pressure distribution and reduce oscillations in the shape parameter $m$.

Curvature visualisation was added as a feature, which made it possible to quickly judge the effect of geometry changes on $c_p$. The scale of this was variable, allowing visualisation of subtle variation such as in Fig. \ref{fig:LLL06}. To examine the curvature at the leading edge, a keyboard shortcut increased the width of the screen.

\section{Model validation}

\subsection{Panel number resolution}

To test the solution fidelity with different panel counts, pressure distributions for a Van de Vooren airfoil were computed. This airfoil geometry is obtained from conformal mapping of a cylinder, therefore an exact analytical solution exists for comparison.

This exact solution is plotted against the model output for various discretisation levels. Low panel counts are most inaccurate close to the suction peak where there is a large adverse pressure gradient. This inaccuracy is more pronounced for the `thin' section than the `thick', as the adverse pressure gradient experienced is stronger. For panel counts larger than 400, the model output matches the exact solution sufficiently well; therefore, 400 panels is chosen for future analysis.

However, these results highlight a weakness of the model. Rather than using evenly spaced panels, it would be advantageous to increase the density close to the leading edge, where a higher resolution is more important. This would enable a lower total panel count without reducing accuracy, thereby improving computation speeds. This approach is already implemented in other industry-standard panel method solvers.

\section{High L/D airfoil design}

This section presents airfoil design iterations aimed at maximising the lift‑to‑drag ratio (L/D). L/D is a measure of aerodynamic efficiency: for a given range, a higher L/D reduces fuel consumption. While the section L/D does not equal the complete aircraft’s L/D (which includes fuselage, tail, and interference drag), it provides an upper bound. Therefore, maximising L/D over the relevant operating conditions is an important goal of airfoil design.

$c_p$ plots are shown for each iteration at $5^{\circ}$ incidence for comparison, a flight typical value that did not cause stall.
Each uses the following symbols: NT, Natural Transition; LS, Laminar Separation; TR, Turbulent Reattachment; TS, Turbulent Separation.

\subsection{Low Re design: $5 \cdot 10^5$}

An airfoil section is optimised for L/D performance at $Re=5 \cdot 10^5$, corresponding to a small glider. At this low Reynolds number, flow will typically be laminar and sensitive to surface imperfections, separating easily. However, if laminar separation can be prevented, L/D gains are made due to the lower drag of a laminar boundary layer \cite{barnard2004aircraft}. Therefore, attached laminar flow over the majority of the airfoil is desirable. However, laminar airfoils tend to have small incidence ranges with separation occuring suddenly \cite{barnard2004aircraft}.
