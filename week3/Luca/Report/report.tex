\subsection{Limitations}

The pressure distribution is calculated entirely from inviscid potential, through a Bernouilli relation with the circulation-derived surface velocities. In reality, the boundary layer displacement thickness affects the outer flow curvature, however no feedback from the boundary layer calculations is implemented. This means that the variation of lift with boundary layer progression (and subsequent stall) is not captured. The effect of laminar separation bubbles on the pressure distribution is also not analysed.

A stagnation point in the trailing edge inviscid flow causes a strong adverse pressure gradient not found empirically. Therefore, it is not enforced in the model. Instead, the extrapolated velocities above and below the leading edge are averaged, to give a more realistic result.

The modelling of transition is also unsophisticated, relying on a simple empirical criterion (Eppler and Somers) that uses only local flow properties. A more physically robust approach is the $e^{n}$ method, which is based on linear stability theory: it tracks the growth of small disturbances (Tollmien–Schlichting waves) as they travel downstream and assumes that transition occurs once these disturbances have amplified by a factor of $e^{n}$. The critical $n$ value depends on the initial turbulence of the flow, typically $n=9$ for wind-tunnel conditions. 

The Thwaites' method is used for laminar flow, which describes the boundary layer shape using a single parameter `m'. However, a two parameter method, such as Head's method \cite{boundarylayersyoung} would be more appropriate. The current method is sensitive to curvature changes and behaves poorly in adverse pressure gradients \cite{oneparameterbad}.

The flow in a laminar separation bubble is considered to transition immediately, as it is not possible to model a separated region using the single-parameter laminar model. However, the position of transition within the bubble has a significant effect on the reattachment position, especially in low Reynolds flow \cite{lowspeeddesign}.

There is no modelling of compressibility, therefore the model is only applicable below Mach 0.3. For the high Reynolds number design case, $Re =2 \cdot 10^7$, this requires a very large aircraft.

\subsection{Comparison with XFoil}

Xfoil is an example of an industry-standard panel method solver. Due to its coupled viscous-inviscid calculations, use of the $e^n$ method for transition tests, two-parameter laminar model and improved treatment of laminar separation bubbles \cite{xfoil}, Xfoil overcomes many of the limitations of our model. Boundary layer growth is accounted for using sources, stall is modelled, and compressibility corrections improve accuracy at higher Mach numbers. It is especially useful for inverse design: the determination of the airfoil shape that would produce a given pressure or velocity distribution.

\subsection{Improvements to \texttt{wasg}}\label{sec:wasg}

Initial attempts to use \texttt{wasg} were challenging, as any `bumpiness' introduced by spline manipulation triggered laminar separation. 

A least-squares spline tool was implemented to resolve this, however the non-monotonic y variation with x was problematic. Splitting the upper and lower surfaces into different splines prevented leading edge smoothness, and fixing this removed the LE and TE position constraint. Instead, we decided to reduce the number of spline `knots'; this also helped to smooth the pressure distribution and reduce oscillations in $m$.

Curvature visualisation was also added, which made it possible to quickly judge the effect of geometry changes on $c_p$. To examine the curvature at the leading edge, a keyboard shortcut increases the width of the screen.

\subsection{Panel number resolution}

To test the variation of solution fidelity with panel number, results for Van de Vooren airfoil were compared. This airfoil geometry is obtained from conformal mapping of a cylinder, therefore an exact analytical solution exists.

This exact solution is plotted against the model output for various panel counts. The model is most inaccurate close to the suction peak where there is a large adverse pressure gradient. This inaccuracy is more pronounced for the 'thin' section than the 'thick', as the adverse pressure gradient experienced is higher. For panel counts larger than 400, the model output matches the exact solution sufficiently well; therefore, future investigations** will use 400 panels.

However, these results highlight a disadvantage of the model. Rather than using evenly spaced panels, it would be advantageous to increase the density close to the leading edge, where a higher resolution has a larger effect. This would allow a lower overall panel count while preserving accuracy, and is implemented in other, industry standard, panel method solvers.

\subsubsection{NACA 0012}

The starting point for this design was the NACA 0012 airfoil described in \ref{sec:naca}, which has a maximum L/D of 58.8 at $7.6^{\circ}$ incidence and $C_L = 0.915$. 

The pressure distribution at $7.6^{\circ}$ shows a strong adverse pressure gradient after the suction peak, with an early laminar separation and turbulent reattachment to form a laminar bubble. Although the flow over the lower surface remains laminar and turbulent separation occurs close to the trailing edge, significant gains can be made by maintaining laminar flow over the upper surface. ** Source?

\subsubsection{LLL01}

The thickness was increased to 15\% to reduce the severity of the suction peak, creating a NACA 0015 equivalent airfoil: LLL01.

The Lift to Drag ratio is slightly lower than for the NACA 0012: 53.3 at $4.9^{\circ}$). The magnitude of the suction peak is reduced, however the post suction peak adverse pressure gradient is still sufficiently high to cause early separation (at comparable incidence).  

Since there is a visible `laminar bucket' region on the $C_D- C_L$ curve that is centered at zero incidence, improvements in L/D can be made by increasing the camber. 

\subsubsection{LLL02}

2\% camber was added with a maximum at 40\% chord, increasing L/D to a maximum of 78.8 at $6.1^{\circ}$, $C_L = 1.005$.

The `laminar bucket' is visibly shifted to positive $C_L$, however laminar separation remains early on the upper surface. 

\subsubsection{LLL03}

In order to reduce the adverse pressure gradient after the leading edge, the maximum thickness point was moved rearward to 35\% chord.

At the incidence at which L/D is maximised ($6^{\circ}$, with L/D = 84.3 and $C_L=0.996$), laminar separation on the upper surface is delayed. 



\subsubsection{LLL04}

The next progression was to increase the camber to 4\%, with the maximum point at 50\% (from 40\%) The thickness has remained unchanged at 15\% with a maximum at 35\% chord.

The $c_p$ distribution is gradually improving and the upper laminar separation point moving rearwards, leading to an increased L/D of 91.4 at $5.6^{\circ}$, $C_L=1.26$.



\subsubsection{LLL05}

Further L/D improvements are reached by increasing camber to  7\%, with a maximum at 50\% chord. Maximum L/D is now 100.4 at $2.3^{\circ}$, $C_L = 1.28$, however the pressure distribution now has a deficiency after the leading edge.

\subsubsection{LLL06}

At this stage, we started to manipulate the geometry manually using the `wasg' tool provided, rather than just camber and thickness. After implementing the improvements in \ref{sec:wasg}, the curvature near the leading edge became smoother due to the reduction in spline knots, which is reflected in the $c_p$ plot.
The aim of geometry changes was to delay separation on the lower surface by smoothing the curvature near the trailing edge. This was achieved, however L/D only increased to 101.3 at $2.9^{\circ}$ and $C_L = 1.34$.

\subsubsection{LLL07}

The priority in this stage was to smooth the leading edge portion of the $c_p$ plot. This was again achieved, with little difference in L/D: 104.3 at $4.6^{\circ}$ and $C_L=1.57$.

\subsubsection{LLL08}

After geometry manipulation at the upper trailing edge, natural transition was achieved. However, turbulent separation also started earlier, with L/D = 112.7 at $3.1^{\circ}$ and $C_L = 1.39$.
